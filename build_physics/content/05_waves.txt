H2 What is a wave?
P A **wave** is a disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another **without transfer of matter**. Examples: sound waves, water waves, light waves.

H3 Terms related to waves
B **Wavelength (λ)**: the distance between successive crests (or two compressions / two rarefactions) of a wave.
B **Frequency (ν or f)**: the number of wave cycles passing a given point in one second; unit hertz (Hz).
B **Time period (T)**: time for one complete cycle; T = 1/f.
B **Amplitude (A)**: maximum displacement measured between the crest and the mid-point (mean position) of the wave.
B **Velocity (v)**: speed and direction of propagation; v = f λ.
B **Intensity**: wave energy passing through unit area per unit time; I = P/A (W/m²).
NOTE The wave terms are printed in Lecture 6 (p. 2) and again as "physical characteristics of a sound wave" in Lecture 7 (pp. 2–3). Generic definitions are kept here; the sound-specific meanings (loudness, pitch) are in Unit 6.

Longitudinal waves need a medium with volume elasticity (so they travel in solids, liquids and gases); transverse mechanical waves need shear elasticity (so they travel only in solids and on liquid surfaces) — that is why earthquake S-waves cannot pass through the Earth's liquid outer core, proving it is liquid.

H2 Electromagnetic waves
B Electromagnetic (EM) waves are a form of energy consisting of **oscillating electric and magnetic fields** that can travel through a vacuum at the speed of light.
B They are created by the **acceleration of charged particles** and travel as **transverse** waves: the electric field (E) and magnetic field (B) are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.
B EM waves are represented by a sinusoidal graph; the highest point is the **crest**, the lowest the **trough**. In vacuum all EM waves travel at **c = 3 × 10^{8} m/s**.

H3 Features of electromagnetic waves (Lecture 6)
B 1. **No medium required** — travel in vacuum (space), unlike sound waves.
B 2. **Transverse nature** — E ⟂ B ⟂ direction of propagation.
B 3. **Speed** — same for all EM waves in vacuum; independent of frequency.
B 4. **Produced by accelerating charges** — moving (oscillating) charged particles generate EM waves.
B 5. **Carry energy and momentum** — can exert pressure (radiation pressure); example: solar radiation.
B 6. **Obey wave phenomena** — reflection, refraction, diffraction, interference (and polarisation).
B 7. **Do not need a material medium** — can travel through empty space (space communication possible).
B 8. **Electric & magnetic fields oscillate** — fields vary sinusoidally and are always in phase.
B 9. **Relationship between speed, frequency and wavelength**: c = ν λ.
B 10. **Wide range of spectrum** — from radio waves to gamma rays; they differ only in wavelength and frequency.

H2 Wave phenomena — a summary (Lecture 7 p. 5)
P Wave phenomena are the various behaviours shown by waves when they interact with media, obstacles or other waves.
B **1. Reflection** — bouncing back of waves from a surface; angle of incidence = angle of reflection; examples: echo (sound), mirror (light).
B **2. Refraction** — bending of waves when they enter a different medium, caused by change in speed; example: sound travelling farther at night (cool air near the ground bends sound waves back towards the surface).
B **3. Diffraction** — bending/spreading of waves around obstacles; more significant when the wavelength is large — sound shows strong diffraction (we hear round corners).
B **4. Interference** — superposition of two or more waves: constructive (increased amplitude) or destructive (decreased amplitude).
B **5. Beats** — produced by the interference of waves of slightly different frequencies; beat frequency = |f_{1} – f_{2}|.
B **6. Doppler effect** — apparent change in frequency due to relative motion between source and observer; examples: ambulance siren, train horn (pitch rises as it approaches, falls as it recedes).
B **7. Standing (stationary) waves** — formed by superposition of two waves travelling in opposite directions; features: **nodes** (zero displacement) and **antinodes** (maximum displacement).
B **8. Polarisation** — restriction of vibrations to a particular direction; **only transverse waves** can be polarised; **sound waves cannot be polarised** (longitudinal).

ADD Doppler effect — the formula and its famous uses (announced in the Lecture-1 plan but never taught) :: Apparent frequency f′ = f (v ± v_{o})/(v ∓ v_{s}) — upper signs for approach (f′ > f), lower for recession. Uses: police speed radar, Doppler ultrasound of blood flow, weather radar, and **red-shift** of galaxies (light from receding galaxies shifts to longer wavelengths — Hubble's evidence for the expanding universe). The effect exists for light as well as sound; a star approaching us looks bluer.
ADD Mach number & shock waves (also in the Lecture-1 plan, not taught) :: Mach number = speed of object ÷ speed of sound in that medium (Mach 1 ≈ 340 m/s in air at sea level). Subsonic < 1, transonic ≈ 1, **supersonic** 1–5, hypersonic > 5. A supersonic object compresses the air ahead into a cone-shaped **shock wave**; the sudden pressure jump heard on the ground is the **sonic boom**. Bullets, Concorde, BrahMos (Mach 2.8–3) are supersonic; hypersonic glide vehicles exceed Mach 5.
