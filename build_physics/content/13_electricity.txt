H2 Electric charge (Lecture 15 p. 2)
P **Electric charge** is the fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical phenomena. It can be **positive (+)** or **negative (–)** and is conserved. It is the property by which matter produces and experiences electrical and magnetic effects; the **excess or deficiency of electrons** in a body is the cause of its net charge (deficiency of electrons → positive; excess → negative).
B **Unit**: coulomb (C); **nature**: scalar; **charge on one electron** = –1.6 × 10^{–19} C (proton +1.6 × 10^{–19} C; 1 C = charge of 6.25 × 10^{18} electrons).
B The charge of fundamental particles (electron, proton) is their internal characteristic, while the charge on a body depends on the number of protons and electrons inside it.
IMGS L15_p2_1 | Field lines of a positive charge (outward) and a negative charge (inward);;L15_p2_charge_forces | (i) Like charges repel — (ii) unlike charges attract
H3 Specific properties of charge
B **Charge is a scalar** — it represents an excess or deficiency of electrons.
B **Charge is transferable** — if a charged body is put in contact with another body, charge can be transferred to it (only electrons move; protons stay in the nucleus).
B **Charge is always associated with mass** — charge cannot exist without mass, though mass can exist without charge (neutron). The presence of charge is itself proof of the existence of mass. **The mass of a body changes after being charged**: a body given a **positive** charge loses electrons and its mass **decreases** (slightly); a body given a **negative** charge gains electrons and its mass **increases**.
B **Charge is quantised** — all free charges are integral multiples of a basic unit e: **q = ne**, n = ±1, ±2, … The quantum of charge is the charge of an electron or proton (Millikan's oil-drop experiment).
B **Charge is conserved** — in an isolated system the total charge does not change with time, though individual charges may change: charge can neither be created nor destroyed. Conservation of charge holds in all chemical (atomic) and nuclear reactions; no exception has ever been found.
B **Charge is invariant** — independent of the frame of reference: the charge on a body does not change whatever its speed (unlike mass).
ADD Methods of charging & electroscope (context frequently asked) :: **Friction** (glass rod rubbed with silk → glass +, silk –; ebonite/plastic with fur → plastic –), **conduction** (contact) and **induction** (charging without contact; the induced charge is opposite). A **gold-leaf electroscope** detects charge (leaves diverge). Charge resides on the **outer surface** of a conductor and concentrates at sharp points (lightning conductor, Unit 14); inside a hollow conductor the field is zero (**Faraday cage** — why a car is safe in lightning). Quarks carry ±e/3 and ±2e/3 but are never free. **Electrostatics in life**: crackling when a nylon sweater is pulled off, hair standing on end with a comb, lightning, photocopiers and laser printers, electrostatic paint spraying, electrostatic precipitators in chimneys, fuel tankers trailing chains to earth static charge.

H2 Coulomb's law (Lecture 15 p. 3)
P The electrostatic force of interaction between two static point charges is **directly proportional to the product of the charges**, **inversely proportional to the square of the distance** between them, and acts along the straight line joining the two charges. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract; the force decreases with distance.
EQ F = k q_{1} q_{2} / r²      k = 1/(4πε_{0}) = 9 × 10^{9} N m² C^{–2}      (ε_{0} = 8.85 × 10^{–12} C² N^{–1} m^{–2}, permittivity of free space)
ADD Coulomb vs Newton, and the medium (context) :: The law has the same inverse-square form as gravitation, but the electric force is ~10^{36} times stronger (Unit 11) and can be attractive *or* repulsive. In a medium the force is *reduced* by the **dielectric constant** K (relative permittivity): F_{medium} = F_{vacuum}/K (water K ≈ 80 — which is why ionic salts dissolve in water; mica ≈ 6, glass ≈ 5–10, metals → ∞). 1 coulomb is an enormous charge — two 1 C charges 1 m apart would repel with 9 × 10^{9} N. The unit is named after **Charles-Augustin de Coulomb** (torsion balance, 1785).

H2 Electric field and field intensity
P An **electric field** is the region around a charge in which another charge experiences a force.
IMG L15_p3_0 | Electric field intensity — the force on a small positive test charge q₀ placed at distance r from Q: E = F/q₀ = kQ/r² | 56
B **Electric field intensity (E)** — the force per unit (positive test) charge: **E = F/q**; unit **N/C** (= V/m); a **vector** in the direction of the force on a positive charge. For a point charge Q at distance r: E = kQ/r².
B **Electric field lines** start on positive charges and end on negative ones, never cross, are crowded where the field is strong, and are perpendicular to a conductor's surface.
ADD Capacitance & capacitors (in the Lecture-1 plan, never taught) :: A **capacitor** stores charge and electrical energy on two conductors separated by an insulator (dielectric). **Capacitance** C = Q/V; unit **farad (F)** (1 F is huge — practical values are μF, nF, pF). Parallel-plate: C = ε_{0}KA/d — larger plates, smaller gap, and a dielectric all *increase* C. Energy stored U = ½CV² = ½QV. Capacitors in **parallel add** (C = C_{1} + C_{2}), in **series** 1/C = 1/C_{1} + 1/C_{2} (the opposite of resistors). Uses: camera flash, tuning circuits in radios, smoothing rectified DC, power-factor correction, defibrillators, keyboards and touch-screens (capacitive sensing), the **Leyden jar** was the first capacitor (1745). A charged capacitor blocks DC but passes AC.

H2 Electric potential and potential difference
IMG L15_p3_1 | Electric potential energy = work done in moving charge 2 from infinity (A) to a finite distance (B) from charge 1 | 50
B **Electric potential (V)** — the work done per unit charge in bringing a positive test charge from infinity to that point: **V = W/q**; unit **volt (V)** — 1 V = 1 joule per coulomb. It is a **scalar**. For a point charge Q: V = kQ/r.
B **Potential difference (voltage)** — the difference in electrical potential energy per unit charge between two points in a circuit; it is what **drives the current**: V_{AB} = W/q. Positive charge flows from higher to lower potential (electrons the other way).
B **Current does not flow between two charged bodies at the same electric potential** — a potential difference is required (Lecture 17 p. 5). This is like heat flowing only between bodies at different temperatures, and water only between different levels.
ADD Potential vs charge — the "big body, small potential" idea (context) :: The Earth is taken as zero potential (earthing, Unit 14). A body at high potential need not have a large charge (a Van de Graaff dome at 10^{6} V holds only microcoulombs), and a cloud with a huge charge may be at modest potential. Birds sit safely on a single high-tension wire because both feet are at the *same* potential — no potential difference, no current. Lightning is a discharge across ~10^{8}–10^{9} V. The **electron-volt** (1 eV = 1.6 × 10^{–19} J) is the energy gained by an electron falling through 1 V. **Equipotential surfaces** are perpendicular to field lines; no work is done in moving a charge along them.

H2 Electric current (Lecture 15 p. 3)
P An **electric current** is a flow of charged particles — electrons or ions — through a conductor or through space; it is the **net rate of flow of electric charge** through a surface.
EQ I = Q / t      unit: **ampere (A)** — 1 A = 1 coulomb per second      (1 A = 6.25 × 10^{18} electrons per second)
B **Direction**: the *conventional* current flows from **+ to –** (the direction positive charge would move); electrons actually drift from – to +.
B Electric current is treated as a **scalar** — it has a direction along the wire but does not obey the vector (parallelogram) law of addition. **Current density** J = I/A (A/m²), however, is a **vector** (69th Q27).
IMGS L15_p2_0 | A simple circuit — battery, switch, wire and bulb: a closed conducting path is needed for a current;;L15_p3_2 | Current = flow of charge q through a cross-section A of the conductor
PYQ 69th Q27: current density → **a vector quantity**. 69th Q19: "Which liquid is a bad conductor of electricity?" — salted water, orange juice and lemon juice are all **electrolytes** (ions carry the current), so the answer is **none of the above**; pure/distilled water, oils and alcohol are the poor conductors.
ADD Drift velocity, and what carries the current (context) :: In a metal the free electrons drift at only ≈ 10^{–4} m/s (a fraction of a millimetre per second), yet the *signal* travels at nearly the speed of light because the field is set up along the whole wire almost instantly — the bulb lights immediately. Charge carriers: **electrons** in metals; **ions (both signs)** in electrolytes and gases (neon tubes, lightning); **electrons and holes** in semiconductors; **protons/ions** in plasma. A current of ≈ 10 mA through the body is painful, ≈ 100 mA across the heart can be fatal — it is the *current*, not the voltage alone, that kills, which is why a 12 V car battery is safe to touch but a 230 V mains socket is not. **Ammeter** measures current (series, low resistance), **voltmeter** measures potential difference (parallel, high resistance) — Unit 14.

H2 Ohm's law (Lecture 16 p. 2)
P At **constant temperature** (and other physical conditions), the current flowing through a conductor is **directly proportional to the potential difference** across its ends.
EQ V = I R      ⇒      I = V/R      ⇒      R = V/I      (R = resistance, unit ohm Ω; 1 Ω = 1 V/A)
IMG L16_p2_0 | Ohm's-law triangle — cover the quantity you want: V = IR, I = V/R, R = V/I | 50
B Valid for **ohmic conductors** (metals, alloys at constant temperature) whose V–I graph is a straight line through the origin; **non-ohmic** devices (diodes, transistors, filament lamps whose temperature changes, electrolytes, thermistors) do not obey it.
PYQ 70th Q104: the resistance of a wire → **R = V/I**. (The other options IV, 1/2V and I/V are traps; learn all three rearrangements from the triangle.)
ADD Georg Simon Ohm (1827) and the graphs (context) :: On a V–I graph the *slope* V/I is the resistance — a steeper line means a larger resistance; on an I–V graph the slope is the *conductance* G = 1/R (unit **siemens**, S, formerly "mho"). A **superconductor** (mercury below 4.2 K — Kamerlingh Onnes, 1911; YBCO ceramics up to ≈ 90 K) has *zero* resistance and expels magnetic fields (Meissner effect) — used in MRI magnets, maglev trains and the LHC.

H2 Classification of materials by conductivity (Lecture 16 p. 2)
IMG L16_p2_1 | Energy bands — insulators (large gap), semiconductors (small gap, few electrons cross at room temperature), conductors (bands overlap) | 58
TBLW 34,33,33
TBL Conductors~Insulators~Semiconductors
Free electrons are present, hence electric current can flow easily (metals — silver, copper, aluminium, gold; also graphite, salt solutions, human body, earth)~Free electrons are **not available**, hence current cannot pass through them (rubber, glass, mica, dry wood, plastics, porcelain, pure/distilled water, dry air, paper, ebonite)~**Few** free electrons are available at room temperature, hence they conduct only slightly (silicon, germanium, gallium arsenide, selenium)
On increasing temperature the flow of current **decreases** (resistance rises — more collisions of electrons with vibrating ions)~Resistance is very high at all ordinary temperatures (breaks down only at very high voltage)~On increasing temperature more free electrons are released, hence the flow of current **increases** (resistance **falls**)
Resistivity 10^{–8} – 10^{–6} Ω m~Resistivity 10^{8} – 10^{16} Ω m~Resistivity 10^{–5} – 10^{3} Ω m (in between)
PYQ 66th Q3: on heating, the resistance of a semiconductor **decreases** (negative temperature coefficient). Contrast: metals — resistance increases on heating; alloys such as manganin and constantan — almost no change (hence used for standard resistors).
ADD Semiconductor extras BPSC touches (photodiode 68th Q113, LEDs, solar cells) :: **Doping** a pure (intrinsic) semiconductor with pentavalent atoms (P, As, Sb) gives an **n-type** (extra electrons); with trivalent atoms (B, Al, Ga, In) a **p-type** (holes). A **p–n junction diode** conducts in one direction only → **rectifier** (AC → DC). **LED** — emits light when forward-biased (Unit 14); **photodiode** — reverse-biased, its current changes with light → light sensors, optical fibre receivers, digital/logic applications (68th Q113); **solar (photovoltaic) cell** — a junction that converts light directly to electricity (silicon); **transistor** (Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley, 1947) — amplifier/switch, the building block of all integrated circuits; **thermistor** — resistance falls steeply with temperature (fever thermometers, fire alarms). **Silver** is the best conductor, then copper, gold, aluminium; **nichrome** is used in heater coils (high resistivity, high melting point), **tungsten** in bulb filaments.

H2 Resistance (Lecture 16 p. 2)
P **Resistance** is the property of a material that opposes the flow of electric current — how difficult it is for electrons to move through a conductor. Unit **ohm (Ω)**.
H3 Factors on which resistance depends
IMG L16_p2_2 | R = ρ L/A — a conductor of length L, cross-section A and resistivity ρ | 52
B **1. Length (L)** — a longer wire has more resistance: **R ∝ L**.
B **2. Area of cross-section (A)** — a thicker wire has less resistance: **R ∝ 1/A**.
B **3. Material (resistivity ρ)** — copper: low resistance; nichrome: high; rubber: very high.
B **4. Temperature** — **metals: resistance increases** with temperature; **semiconductors (and insulators, electrolytes): resistance decreases** with temperature.
EQ R = ρ L / A      (ρ = resistivity / specific resistance, unit Ω m — a property of the material and temperature only, not of the dimensions)
CORR Lecture 16 prints the resistivity symbol as "δ" in "R = δℓ/A" and as "p" in the text; the standard symbol is ρ (rho). Conductivity σ = 1/ρ (unit S/m).
ADD Stretching and folding a wire (the standard numerical) :: The **volume** of a wire stays constant, so if it is stretched to n times its length, A becomes 1/n of the original and R becomes **n²** times (doubling the length by stretching → 4R); if a wire is folded/cut into n equal parts and bundled in parallel, R becomes R/n². Doubling the *diameter* (same length) makes R one-quarter. Resistivity order (room temperature): silver 1.6 × 10^{–8} Ω m < copper 1.7 × 10^{–8} < gold < aluminium 2.8 × 10^{–8} < tungsten 5.6 × 10^{–8} < iron ≈ 10^{–7} < nichrome ≈ 10^{–6} < germanium ≈ 0.5 < silicon ≈ 2000 < glass ≈ 10^{12}. **Rheostat** = variable resistance (used to control current in a circuit, e.g. fan regulator); colour code of carbon resistors: B B ROY of Great Britain had a Very Good Wife (0–9).

H2 Combination of resistors (Lecture 16 pp. 2–3)
P Two or more resistors may be connected in **series**, in **parallel**, or in a combination of both, to provide a specific total resistance, control the current and distribute voltage appropriately across the parts of a circuit.
IMGS L16_p3_0 | Series resistors — the same current flows through each; the voltage divides; R = R₁ + R₂ + … + R_n;;L16_p3_1 | Parallel resistors — the same voltage across each; the current divides; 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … + 1/R_n
H3 I. Series combination
B Resistors connected one after another in a single path, so that the current has **only one path**; the **same current flows through each** resistor (charge leaving one enters the next without branching).
B The total potential difference is shared: V = V_{1} + V_{2} + V_{3} (larger R takes the larger share, V ∝ R).
EQ R_{s} = R_{1} + R_{2} + R_{3} + …      (equivalent resistance is **greater** than the greatest individual resistance)
B If one element fails the whole circuit is broken — that is why old fairy lights all went out together, and why fuses and switches are connected in series.
H3 II. Parallel combination
B Resistors connected between the **same two points**, giving **multiple paths**; the **potential difference across each is the same**, while the total current **divides** among the branches: I = I_{1} + I_{2} + … (the smaller R takes the larger current, I ∝ 1/R).
EQ 1/R_{p} = 1/R_{1} + 1/R_{2} + 1/R_{3} + …      for two resistors  R_{p} = R_{1}R_{2}/(R_{1} + R_{2})      (equivalent resistance is **less** than the smallest individual resistance)
B n equal resistors R: series → nR; parallel → R/n. Domestic appliances are wired in **parallel** so that each gets the full mains voltage and works independently (Unit 14).
PYQ 66th Q2: two parallel resistors give 1.403 kΩ, one is 2.0 kΩ → 1/R_{2} = 1/1.403 – 1/2 = 0.7128 – 0.5 = 0.2128 ⇒ R_{2} = **4.70 kΩ** (check: 2 × 4.7/6.7 = 1.403 ✓).
ADD Cells in series and parallel, EMF and internal resistance (standard follow-up) :: **EMF (E)** of a cell = the potential difference across its terminals when no current is drawn; **terminal voltage** V = E – Ir, where r is the cell's **internal resistance** (a new dry cell ≈ 1.5 V, r ≈ 0.1–1 Ω; a car battery 12 V, r ≈ 0.01 Ω — hence it can deliver hundreds of amperes). Cells in **series** add their EMFs (a torch with two 1.5 V cells gives 3 V); identical cells in **parallel** give the same EMF but can supply more current for longer. **Kirchhoff's laws**: the sum of currents at a junction is zero (charge conservation); the sum of EMFs round a loop equals the sum of IR drops (energy conservation). **Wheatstone bridge** (P/Q = R/S when the galvanometer shows zero) measures an unknown resistance — the **metre bridge** is its laboratory form; the **potentiometer** compares EMFs without drawing current (Unit 14).
ADD Worked examples in the recent-BPSC style :: (1) 2 Ω, 3 Ω and 6 Ω in parallel: 1/R = 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6 = 1 ⇒ R = **1 Ω**; in series 11 Ω. (2) A 230 V heater draws 4.6 A: R = 50 Ω, P = 1058 W. (3) Three 60 Ω bulbs in parallel across 12 V: each draws 0.2 A, total 0.6 A, equivalent 20 Ω. (4) A wire of 8 Ω is stretched to double its length → 32 Ω; cut into four equal pieces connected in parallel → 0.5 Ω. (5) A 12 V battery of internal resistance 0.5 Ω across 5.5 Ω: I = 12/6 = 2 A, terminal voltage 11 V.
