H2 Units and measurement
B To measure a physical quantity like length, mass and time we require a **standard of measurement**. This standard is called the **unit** of that physical quantity — e.g. the unit of length is the metre, and a standard length of 1 metre has a precise definition.
B To measure the length of an object we determine how many times this standard metre is contained in the length of the object.
B **Measurement** = the comparison of a physical quantity with a standard quantity.

H3 Physical quantities
B Those quantities which can describe the laws of physics are called physical quantities. A physical quantity is one that can be measured — length, mass, time, pressure, temperature, current and resistance are physical quantities.
B **Fundamental (base) quantities**: physical quantities that are independent of each other.
B **Derived quantities**: all other quantities, which can be expressed in terms of the fundamental quantities.

H3 Units — properties, types and systems
P **Unit** = the reference standard used to measure a physical quantity. A good unit:
B should be of suitable size and well-defined;
B should be easily reproducible (should not change with place) and must not change with time;
B should not change with physical conditions like temperature, pressure, etc.;
B must be easily comparable experimentally with similar physical quantities.
B **Fundamental units** — units defined for the fundamental quantities. **Derived units** — units of all other quantities, derived from the fundamental units.

H3 Definitions of the base units (as given in the lecture)
B **Metre (m)**: the distance travelled by light in vacuum during a time interval of 1/299 792 458 second.
B **Kilogram (kg)**: the mass of a platinum-iridium cylinder kept at the International Bureau of Weights and Measures, Sèvres (Paris).
B **Second (s)**: the time taken by the light of a specified wavelength emitted by a caesium-133 atom to execute 9 192 631 770 vibrations.
B **Ampere (A)**: the current which, when passed through two straight parallel conductors of infinite length and negligible cross-section kept 1 metre apart in vacuum, produces between them a force of 2 × 10^{–7} newton per metre length.
B **Kelvin (K)**: the fraction 1/273.16 of the thermodynamic temperature of the triple point of water.
B **Candela (cd)**: 1/60th of the luminous intensity of 1 square centimetre of a perfect black body maintained at the freezing temperature of platinum (1773 °C).
B **Mole (mol)**: the amount of substance that contains as many elementary units as there are atoms in 0.012 kg of carbon-12.
B **Radian (rad)**: the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc whose length equals the radius.
B **Steradian (sr)**: the solid angle subtended at the centre of a sphere by a surface of area equal to the square of its radius.
CORR Source prints "kept at the National Bureau of Weights and Measurements, Paris" (it is the *International* Bureau, BIPM), "1/273.6 of the thermodynamic temperature" (correct fraction is **1/273.16**), and "kept at 1A. meter apart" (read: 1 metre apart).
ADD 2019 redefinition of the SI (context, not in the lectures) :: Since 20 May 2019 all seven base units are defined through fixed values of natural constants — the kilogram via Planck's constant h = 6.626 070 15 × 10^{–34} J s (Kibble balance), the ampere via the elementary charge e = 1.602 176 634 × 10^{–19} C, the kelvin via Boltzmann's constant k = 1.380 649 × 10^{–23} J/K and the mole via Avogadro's number 6.022 140 76 × 10^{23}. The platinum-iridium cylinder ("Le Grand K") is therefore no longer the definition of the kilogram, though the lecture's older definitions are still what most objective papers quote.

H2 Dimensional formula
P The dimensional formula of a physical quantity tells which of the fundamental units have been used for its measurement, and to what power.
H3 How is the dimensional formula of a physical quantity written?
B (1) Write the formula of the quantity, with the quantity on the left-hand side.
B (2) Express all quantities on the right-hand side in terms of fundamental quantities — mass, length and time.
B (3) Replace mass, length and time by M, L and T respectively.
B (4) Write the powers of the terms.
NOTE Lecture 19 prints this introduction and the four-step question twice (bottom of p. 3 and top of p. 4). Kept once.

H3 Characteristics of dimensions
B Dimensions do not depend on the system of units.
B Only quantities with the same dimensions can be added or subtracted.
B Dimensions can be obtained from the units of the physical quantity and vice versa.
B Two different quantities can have the same dimension (e.g. work and torque).
B When two quantities are multiplied or divided, their dimensions multiply/divide to give the dimension of the third quantity.
H3 Uses of dimensional formulae
B (1) To check the correctness of an equation. (2) To convert a unit from one system to another. (3) To deduce the relation connecting physical quantities.

ADD Handy unit facts BPSC keeps asking (not in the lecture text) :: 1 Å (angstrom) = 10^{–10} m — a unit of **wavelength/atomic size** (64th Q3); 1 light-year = distance light travels in one year ≈ 9.46 × 10^{15} m — a unit of **distance**, not time (66th Q5); 1 parsec ≈ 3.26 light-years; 1 astronomical unit (AU) ≈ 1.496 × 10^{11} m (Earth–Sun distance); 1 fermi = 10^{–15} m (nuclear size); 1 micron = 10^{–6} m; 1 nautical mile = 1852 m; 1 bar = 10^{5} Pa ≈ 1 atm (1 atm = 1.013 × 10^{5} Pa = 760 mm Hg); 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10^{6} J; 1 eV = 1.6 × 10^{–19} J; 1 hp = 746 W; 1 calorie = 4.186 J; 1 dyne = 10^{–5} N; 1 erg = 10^{–7} J.
ADD Quantities with no unit / same dimensions (exam favourites) :: **Dimensionless**: strain, refractive index, relative density (specific gravity), coefficient of friction, Poisson's ratio, angle (radian is a ratio), efficiency, magnification, dielectric constant (relative permittivity), relative permeability. **Same dimension pairs**: work–energy–torque–heat (M L^{2}T^{–2}); impulse–momentum (M L T^{–1}); pressure–stress–Young's modulus–energy density (M L^{–1}T^{–2}); frequency–angular velocity–velocity gradient–decay constant (T^{–1}); Planck's constant–angular momentum (M L^{2}T^{–1}); force constant–surface tension (M T^{–2}).
