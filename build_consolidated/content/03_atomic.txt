H2 3.1 Foundations — Laws of Chemical Combination
NOTE The "What is atomic structure?" introduction of the source opens with the mass laws; they are kept here in full.
H3 Law of Multiple Proportions — John Dalton
B If two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a ratio of **small whole numbers**. e.g. in CO and CO₂, fixed carbon (12 g) combines with 16 g and 32 g of oxygen — a simple **1 : 2** ratio.
B Does not apply to non-stoichiometric compounds, polymers or oligomers; inapplicable for isotopes. It solidified the concept of atoms and helped determine relative atomic masses.
H3 Law of Definite Proportions — Joseph Proust (1799)
B A pure chemical compound always contains the **same elements in the same fixed proportion by mass**, regardless of source, method of preparation or quantity.
B Not applicable to compounds containing isotopes (e.g. ³⁵Cl₂ vs ³⁷Cl₂) and non-stoichiometric compounds.
H3 Law of Conservation of Mass — Antoine Lavoisier
B Matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction or physical change; **mass of reactants = mass of products** (atoms are only rearranged). Basis of balancing equations and stoichiometry.
B Holds only for low-energy chemical reactions — in nuclear reactions mass converts to energy.

H2 3.2 Dalton's Atomic Theory
B John Dalton: all matter is made of **atoms** — indivisible and indestructible; atoms of an element are exactly the same, atoms of different elements differ in size and mass.
B **Postulates:**
BB Every matter is made up of atoms; atoms are indivisible.
BB Specific elements have only one type of atom; each atom has its own constant mass that varies element to element.
BB Atoms undergo rearrangement during a chemical reaction; atoms can neither be created nor destroyed but transformed.
B Successfully explained the laws of chemical combination — conservation of mass, constant properties, multiple proportions and reciprocal proportions.
B **Demerits:** could not explain isotopes; said nothing about the structure of the atom; later sub-atomic particles proved atoms are divisible.

H2 3.3 Thomson's Model (Plum-Pudding)
B Postulate — the atom is a **sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded** in it.
B Limitation — could not satisfactorily explain the results of Rutherford's scattering experiment.

H2 3.4 Rutherford's Model (Nuclear Model)
B Experiment — thin **gold foil** bombarded with fast **alpha (α) particles**; he expected the gold atoms to deflect them.
IMG p033_1|Scattering of alpha particles by the gold foil|56
TBL Observation~Inference
Most α-particles passed straight through the gold foil~Atom contains a lot of empty space
Some particles diverted by small angles~Positive charges do not occupy much of the atom's space
Only 1 out of ~12000 particles bounced back~Positive charge is concentrated in a very small area (nucleus)
TBLW 52,48
B **Nuclear model:** atoms contain a lot of unoccupied space; a heavy **positively charged nucleus** sits at the centre; electrons revolve around it in circular paths called **orbits**; nucleus is much smaller than the atom and holds almost all the mass.
B **Drawback (Maxwell's objection):** a charged particle moving in a circle radiates energy — the electron would lose speed and spiral into the nucleus, so the atom would be unstable. The model could not explain atomic stability.
B **Nucleons** — protons and neutrons collectively.

H2 3.5 Bohr's Model of the Atom
IMG p034_0|Bohr's model — shells K, L, M, N as energy levels|42
B Electrons spin around the nucleus only in **certain separate allowed orbits (energy levels)** — they do **not** emit energy while in these special orbits. Orbits are labelled K, L, M, N… (n = 1, 2, 3, 4…).
B Electron absorbs energy as EMR when it jumps from a lower energy level (ground state) to a higher one (excited state) and vice-versa: **ΔE = E₂ − E₁ = hν**.
B Quantitative results for hydrogen-like species (H, He⁺, Li²⁺, nuclear charge Z):
EQ Radius of n-th orbit:  rₙ = 52.9 × n²/Z pm
EQ Energy of n-th orbit:  Eₙ = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ (Z²/n²) J = −13.6 (Z²/n²) eV = −313.6 (Z²/n²) kcal/mol
EQ Velocity of electron:  v = (2.18 × 10⁸) Z/n cm s⁻¹        (n = 1, 2, 3, 4…)
B **Limitations of Bohr's theory:** failed for atoms with more than one electron; could not explain splitting of spectral lines in strong magnetic (Zeeman) or electric (Stark) fields; ignored dual nature of particles and the uncertainty principle.

H2 3.7 Atomic Number, Mass Number & Electron Distribution
B **Atomic number (Z)** = number of protons in an atom. Atoms of different elements always have different proton counts; elements are defined by Z (hydrogen: Z = 1).
B **Mass number (A)** = number of protons + number of neutrons.
H3 Rules for Electron Distribution
B (i) An orbit can hold a maximum of **2n²** electrons; (ii) orbits fill from inside out (n = 1 first); (iii) the outermost shell cannot hold more than **8 electrons** even if it has capacity — the **Octet rule** (8 electrons in the outermost shell makes the atom very stable).
IMG p028_0|Rules for electron distribution|62
TBL Electron shell~Maximum capacity
K shell (n = 1)~2(1)² = 2 electrons
L shell (n = 2)~2(2)² = 8 electrons
M shell (n = 3)~2(3)² = 18 electrons
N shell (n = 4)~2(4)² = 32 electrons

H3 Valency
B **Valence electrons** — electrons in the outermost orbit (valence shell). Valency rises 1→4 then falls to 0 across a period and **remains the same down a group**.
B **Stable configuration:** 2 e⁻ if K is the outermost shell, otherwise 8 e⁻. Atoms react to achieve it; noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) are inert as they already have it.
B Valency = electrons an atom must give away or take to attain a stable configuration. Outermost-shell electrons decide it: silver has 1 outer electron → valency 1. Helium (2 outer electrons) can share both → valency 2. If the outer shell is nearly full, valency = 8 − outer electrons (oxygen: 8 − 6 = 2).

H2 3.8 Isotopes, Isobars & Atomic Species
H3 Isotopes
B Atoms of the **same element** with the **same atomic number but different mass numbers**; pure substances; similar chemical nature but distinct physical characteristics.
IMG p036_0|Isotopes of hydrogen — protium, deuterium, tritium|46
B **Uses of isotopes:** fuel of nuclear reactors — isotope of **uranium**; treatment of cancer — isotope of **cobalt**; treatment of goitre — isotope of **iodine**.
H3 Isobars
B Atoms of **different elements** with the **same mass number** but different atomic numbers (e.g. ¹⁸Ar, ¹⁹K, ²⁰Ca — all A = 40).
IMG p036_1|Isobars — same A, different Z|52
H3 Atomic species to remember
TBL Terms~Definition / explanation
Atomic number (Z)~Number of protons (or electrons) of a neutral atom
Mass number (A)~Total number of protons and neutrons
Nucleons~Protons + neutrons present in the nucleus
Isotopes~Same Z, different A — e.g. ₁H¹, ₁H², ₁H³
Isobars~Same A, different Z — e.g. ₁₅P³² and ₁₆S³²
Isotones~Same number of neutrons, different Z or A — e.g. ₆C¹⁴, ₈O¹⁶, ₇N¹⁵
Isoelectronic~Atoms/molecules/ions with the same electron count — e.g. N₂, CO, CN⁻
Nuclear isomers~Same Z and A but different radioactive properties — U-X (half-life 1.4 min) and U-Z (half-life 6.7 h)
Isosters~Molecules with same number of atoms and same number of electrons — e.g. N₂ and CO

H2 3.10 Planck's Quantum Theory & Photoelectric Effect
B Substances radiate or absorb energy **discontinuously** in the form of energy packets; the smallest packet is a **quantum** (in light: **photon**).
B E ∝ ν → **E = hν**, h = Planck's constant = 6.626 × 10⁻²⁷ erg·s = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. A body radiates/absorbs whole-number multiples: hν, 2hν, 3hν … nhν.
B **Photoelectric effect:** light of suitable frequency ejects electrons from a metal surface; minimum frequency needed = **threshold frequency (ν₀)**; kinetic energy of ejected electrons ∝ frequency; number of electrons per second ∝ intensity of radiation.
EQ hν − hν₀ = ½mv²

H2 3.11 Towards the Quantum Model
H3 de-Broglie equation
EQ λ = h/mv = h/p      (dual nature of matter)
H3 Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle
EQ Δx · Δp ≥ h / 4πm
B It is impossible to determine simultaneously the exact position and exact momentum of an electron.
H3 Quantum Numbers
TBL Quantum number~What it tells
Principal (n)~Main shell & approximate distance from nucleus; max electrons in shell = 2n²
Azimuthal (l)~Number of subshells in the shell (s, p, d, f); their shapes; for given n, l = 0 … (n−1)
Magnetic (m)~Preferred orientations of the electron in a subshell; for given l, m = −l … +l (2l+1 values)
Spin (s)~Direction of spin; only two values, −½ and +½

H3 Rules for Filling of Electrons
B **Aufbau principle** — electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy; lower (n+l) means lower energy.
B **Pauli's exclusion principle** — an orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins.
B **Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity** — pairing in p, d, f orbitals cannot start until each orbital of the subshell is singly occupied.
IMG p040_0|Aufbau energy order 1s → 7s|52
B Increasing energy order: 1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d…
H3 Exceptional Configurations — stability of half/full-filled orbitals
B Cu (29 electrons): expected 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁹, but shifting one electron from 4s to 3d gives a symmetrical, more stable distribution → actual configuration **1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s¹ 3d¹⁰**.
B Fully-filled and half-filled orbitals are extra stable — the same rule explains Cr and other exceptions.
