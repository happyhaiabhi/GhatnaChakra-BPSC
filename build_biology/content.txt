H2 Classification: the organising logic
B Aristotle grouped animals as **Enaima** (red blood present) and **Anaima** (red blood absent).
B Carl Linnaeus used the two-kingdom arrangement: **Plantae** and **Animalia**.
B R. H. Whittaker's five kingdoms are **Monera, Protista, Fungi/Mycota, Plantae and Animalia**. The stated bases are cell complexity, cellular organisation, cell wall and mode of nutrition.
B **Monera** includes prokaryotes such as bacteria and Mycoplasma. **Protista** includes unicellular eukaryotes such as protozoans, Chlamydomonas, Euglena, diatoms and yeast in the source scheme. Multicellular eukaryotes separate broadly into Plantae (cell wall; mainly autotrophic), Fungi/Mycota (cell wall; heterotrophic) and Animalia (no cell wall; heterotrophic).

H2 Cell theory and discovery
P The **cell** is the structural and functional unit of life. All organisms are cellular, and new cells arise from pre-existing cells.
B Robert Hooke observed cork compartments and named “cells” in **1665**.
B Antonie van Leeuwenhoek first observed living cells/microorganisms.
B Cells are classified as **prokaryotic** or **eukaryotic** mainly by the presence of a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

UNIT 2|Cell Boundaries & Organelles|L2–L4|Cell Biology
H2 Cell wall and plasma membrane
H3 Cell wall
B A non-living, rigid layer outside the plasma membrane in plant cells; it provides shape, support and protection and resists osmotic bursting.
B **Plants:** cellulose, hemicellulose, pectins and proteins. **Algae:** composition varies and may include cellulose, galactans, mannans and calcium carbonate. **Fungi:** chitin and glucans.
B **Middle lamella:** mainly calcium pectate; cements adjacent cells. **Primary wall:** extensible wall formed during growth. **Secondary wall:** deposited inward after growth, often thick and strengthened.
B The wall protects against mechanical stress, physical shock and many pathogens while remaining porous to water and small solutes.
IMG leaf_anatomy:Leaf cross-section showing epidermis, mesophyll, vascular tissue and stomata|62

H3 Plasma membrane — fluid mosaic model
B A thin, living, flexible and **selectively permeable** boundary found in every cell; it separates cytoplasm from the external environment and maintains homeostasis.
B Singer and Nicolson proposed the **fluid mosaic model (1972)**.
B Phospholipids form a bilayer: hydrophilic heads face aqueous surfaces; hydrophobic tails face inward. Cholesterol, proteins and carbohydrates occur within or on the bilayer.
B **Integral/intrinsic proteins** are partly or wholly embedded; **peripheral/extrinsic proteins** lie on the surface. Lateral mobility supports growth, junction formation, secretion and endocytosis.
B The membrane regulates movement of nutrients, oxygen, ions and wastes, receives signals and supports cell recognition.

H3 Nucleus
B Robert Brown described the nucleus (1831/1833 in different source references). The outer nuclear membrane is continuous with ER.
B Nuclear pores control RNA and protein traffic. Nucleoplasm contains chromatin and one or more nucleoli.
B The **nucleolus** is non-membranous, rich in rRNA and protein, and is the site of rRNA synthesis and ribosomal-subunit assembly.
B **Chromatin = DNA + histone and associated proteins**; it condenses into chromosomes during division.
B Ribosomes were characterised by George Palade and are non-membranous. Bacterial/organellar **70S = 50S + 30S**; eukaryotic cytosolic **80S = 60S + 40S**. The Svedberg values describe sedimentation and are not additive as ordinary numbers.
B A mature plant central vacuole may occupy around 90% of cell volume; small temporary vacuoles/vesicles occur in animal cells.
B Theodor Boveri is associated with discovery/description of the centrosome. It duplicates with the cell cycle and organises spindle microtubules and astral rays.
IMGS nucleus:Nucleus—envelope, pores, chromatin, nucleolus and nucleoplasm|centrosome:Centrosome and centrioles organising the spindle

H3 Mitochondria
B Usually sausage-shaped or cylindrical; source dimensions are about 0.2–1.0 µm in diameter and 1.0–4.1 µm long.
B Smooth outer membrane surrounds an inner membrane folded into **cristae**, which increase area for electron transport and ATP synthesis. The **matrix** contains enzymes, mtDNA and ribosomes.
B Semi-autonomous: own circular DNA, RNA and 70S ribosomes, but depends heavily on nuclear genes.
B Discovery notes retained: Richard Altmann recognised “bioblasts” (1894); Carl Benda coined *mitochondria* (1898).
IMG mitochondrion:Mitochondrial membranes, cristae, matrix, mtDNA and ATP synthase|62

H3 Endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi and lysosome
B ER partitions the cell into luminal and cytosolic compartments. It is absent in prokaryotes and mature mammalian RBCs.
B **RER:** abundant in protein-secreting cells. **SER:** abundant where lipids/steroids are made and drugs are detoxified.
B Golgi “post office”: the **cis face** receives ER vesicles; cisternae process cargo; the **trans face** dispatches it. Golgi also contributes to lysosomes, sperm acrosome and plant cell plate.
B Camillo Golgi observed the Golgi apparatus in 1897/1898. Lysosomes arise through the endomembrane system, contain many acid hydrolases and function near **pH 5**.

UNIT 3|Nucleic Acids, Genetic Code & Protein Synthesis|L6, L32|Genetics, DNA & Biotechnology
H2 Building blocks
B Friedrich Miescher isolated an acidic nuclear substance from pus cells in 1869 and called it **nuclein**. Altmann popularised the term **nucleic acid**.
B Nucleic acids are nucleotide polymers. **Nucleoside = nitrogenous base + pentose sugar**; **nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate**.
B **Purines** have two rings: adenine (A), guanine (G). **Pyrimidines** have one ring: cytosine (C), thymine (T), uracil (U).
B A base joins carbon 1′ of sugar: N1 of a pyrimidine or N9 of a purine. Phosphate is acidic and negatively charged.
B Pentoses are deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA.
IMGS dna_structure:DNA double helix and base-pair legend|rna_structure:RNA strand, sugar-phosphate backbone and bases

H2 DNA model and functions
B DNA is a long, negatively charged polymer of deoxyribonucleotides and is the hereditary material in almost all cellular organisms.
B Watson and Crick proposed the double-helix model in **1953**, drawing critically on X-ray evidence associated with Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins. Watson, Crick and Wilkins received the 1962 Nobel Prize.
B Two polynucleotide strands are **antiparallel** (5′→3′ opposite 3′→5′), complementary and held by hydrogen bonds and base stacking.
B A pairs with T through **2 H-bonds**; G pairs with C through **3 H-bonds**. Pairing one purine with one pyrimidine keeps helix width nearly uniform.
B Functions: long-term information storage; self-replication before cell division; directing RNA and protein synthesis; mutation as a source of variation and evolution.

H2 Central dogma and protein synthesis
P **DNA → RNA → protein** summarises the usual flow of sequence information. Replication copies DNA; transcription makes RNA from DNA; translation reads mRNA to assemble a polypeptide.
B **Transcription:** RNA polymerase uses one DNA strand as template and makes complementary RNA 5′→3′.
B **Translation:** a ribosome reads mRNA codons; tRNAs match anticodons and deliver amino acids; peptide bonds form a polypeptide.
B Protein synthesis supplies enzymes, structural proteins and many peptide hormones; it underpins growth, repair and metabolic regulation.
IMG transcription_translation:Transcription of DNA to RNA followed by translation to protein|68

H2 Genetic code
B George Gamow advanced the triplet-code idea; Nirenberg, Matthaei and Khorana were central to deciphering the code.
B A **codon** is a three-base mRNA sequence specifying an amino acid or stop signal. Four bases in triplets yield 4³ = **64 codons**, enough for 20 standard amino acids.
B **AUG** encodes methionine and commonly initiates translation. **UAA, UAG and UGA** are stop/nonsense codons.
B The code is degenerate (most amino acids have multiple codons), unambiguous for a given codon, nearly universal, non-overlapping and read in a fixed frame.

H2 Cell cycle overview
B Interphase is an active preparation period: G1 growth, S DNA replication, G2 preparation. Division phase includes nuclear division (**karyokinesis**) and cytoplasmic division (**cytokinesis**).
B **Mitosis** is equational division: one parent cell → two genetically similar cells with the same chromosome number.
B **Meiosis** includes one DNA replication followed by two divisions: one diploid germ cell → four genetically varied haploid cells.

H2 Mendel's laws
B Mendel worked with garden pea (*Pisum sativum*) and seven pairs of contrasting traits. His work established particulate inheritance.
B **Dominance:** in a simple heterozygote, the dominant allele determines the F1 phenotype.
B **Segregation / purity of gametes:** two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation; each gamete receives one.
B **Independent assortment:** alleles of different genes assort independently when genes are unlinked; a classic dihybrid F2 gives **9:3:3:1**.
B Pea contrasts retained: seed shape round/wrinkled; seed colour yellow/green; flower colour violet/white; pod shape inflated/constricted; pod colour green/yellow; flower position axial/terminal; stem tall/dwarf.
IMG mendel_traits:Mendel's seven contrasting pea characters|72

H3 Sex determination and modern applications
B In humans the ovum always carries X, whereas sperm carries X or Y. Fertilisation by X-bearing sperm usually produces XX and by Y-bearing sperm XY; therefore the sperm determines chromosomal sex in the textbook model.
B **Mutation** is a change in DNA sequence and is a source of variation. **Biotechnology/genetic engineering** manipulates DNA for medicine, agriculture and research.
B **DNA fingerprinting/profiling** compares highly variable DNA markers to support identity, forensic investigation and biological-parentage analysis; statistical interpretation is essential.
ADDED Modern qualification|Chromosomal sex determination is more complex in development than “XX female/XY male” alone; the line above is the standard BPSC model and exceptions exist.

H2 Evolution
B Evolution is heritable change in populations over generations.
B **Lamarck:** use/disuse and inheritance of acquired characters; historically important but not the accepted general mechanism.
B **Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace:** overproduction → variation → struggle for existence → differential survival/reproduction (natural selection) → adaptation.
B The modern synthesis combines natural selection with mutation, recombination, gene flow and genetic drift as causes of evolutionary change.
IMG lamarck_darwin:Lamarckian and Darwinian explanations illustrated with giraffes|66

H3 Evidence
B **Fossils/palaeontology:** ordered remains in rock strata document succession and transitional forms.
B **Homologous structures:** common origin and basic plan, potentially different functions—forelimbs of human, whale, bat and cheetah; vertebrate hearts/brains; insect mouthparts; testes and ovaries; potato and ginger as shoots; radish and carrot as roots; molecular similarities.
B **Analogous structures:** different origin/plan but similar function—bird and insect wings; octopus and mammal eyes; penguin and dolphin flippers; sweet-potato root and potato stem as storage organs; bee and scorpion stings.
B **Vestigial structures:** reduced remnants—nictitating membrane, auricular muscles, vermiform appendix, coccyx, reduced canine/wisdom teeth, body hair, male nipples and segmented abdominal muscles.

H2 Algae
B Green algae: chlorophyll a and b; examples Chlorella, Chara, Cladophora, Volvox, Ulva and Acetabularia.
B Brown algae: fucoxanthin dominant; Fucus, Dictyota, Laminaria and kelps (commercial iodine source).

H2 Bryophytes
B Earliest land-plant lineages derived from green-algal ancestors; dependent on water for fertilisation because male gametes are motile.
B Lack true roots and vascular tissue, so remain small; water conduction is largely cell-to-cell/parenchymatous. They favour moist, shady places (**sciophytes**).
B Bryophyta term credited in the source to Robert Braun; study is **bryology**. Hedwig is widely called father of bryology; the source also mentions Cavers, and Prof. Shiv Ram Kashyap as father of Indian bryology.
B Main classes shown: liverworts (Hepaticopsida), hornworts (Anthocerotopsida), mosses (Bryopsida).

H2 Pteridophytes
B Called vascular cryptogams and sometimes “reptiles of the plant kingdom.” They have roots and vascular tissue but still need water for fertilisation.
B Typical xylem lacks vessels and phloem lacks companion cells. Primary root is often short-lived and replaced by adventitious roots; underground stem may be a rhizome.
B **Psilopsida:** primitive vascular plants; Psilotum. **Lycopsida:** club mosses with microphylls and strobili; Lycopodium, Selaginella. **Sphenopsida:** horsetails with jointed stems and strobili; Equisetum. **Pteropsida:** ferns with large fronds, young leaves circinately coiled.
B Fern examples: Azolla (small aquatic fern, biofertiliser); Adiantum (maidenhair/walking fern; leaf-tip propagation); Alsophila (tree fern); Pteridium, Dryopteris, Marsilea, Pteris, Salvinia. Marsilea, Azolla and Salvinia form sporocarps.
B Giant ancient pteridophyte vegetation contributed substantially to coal-forming deposits.

H2 Gymnosperms
B Ovules are not enclosed by an ovary, so seeds remain “naked” and fruits do not form. Roots are usually tap roots; stem may be unbranched (Cycas) or branched (Pinus, Cedrus).
B Pinus has mycorrhizal roots; Cycas has coralloid roots with nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria.
B Conifers reduce water loss with needle leaves, thick cuticle and sunken stomata. Many are xerophytic and occur on Himalayan slopes.
B Typical gymnosperm xylem lacks vessels and phloem lacks sieve tubes/companion cells; **Gnetum, Ephedra and Welwitschia** are vessel-bearing exceptions cited.
B Secondary growth makes most woody/arborescent; Ephedra may be shrubby and Gnetum ula a woody climber.

H2 Angiosperms
B Flowers bear pollen and ovules; ovules become seeds enclosed by ovary-derived fruits. Angiosperms range from tiny Wolffia to Eucalyptus over 100 m and supply food, fodder, fuel, medicines and commercial products.
B Two classic classes: monocotyledons and dicotyledons.

UNIT 8|Plant Physiology, Mineral Nutrition & Diseases|L8, L26, L27|Plant Form, Function & Classification
H2 Photosynthesis
P Green plants convert light energy to chemical energy, reducing CO₂ to carbohydrate and releasing O₂ from water. It occurs principally in chloroplasts.
B Overall classroom equation: **6CO₂ + 12H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O** in the presence of chlorophyll.
B Joseph Priestley showed that plants restore air; Jan Ingenhousz established the role of light and green parts. Melvin Calvin mapped the carbon-reduction cycle; Robert Hill demonstrated light-driven oxygen release in isolated chloroplasts.
B **Light reactions** on thylakoid membranes use photosystems, electron carriers and ATP synthase; water is split, O₂ is released, and ATP/NADPH form.
B **Calvin cycle** in stroma fixes CO₂ using Rubisco, reduces intermediates and regenerates RuBP; ATP and NADPH are consumed.

H2 Respiration in plants
P Plants respire continuously. Gas exchange is by diffusion through stomata, lenticels and root surfaces; no specialised respiratory organ or long-distance gas-transport system is required.
B **Glycolysis:** cytosol; glucose → 2 pyruvate + net 2 ATP + 2 NADH.
B **Aerobic respiration:** pyruvate oxidation and Krebs/TCA cycle in mitochondrial matrix; electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation on inner membrane. O₂ is final electron acceptor.
B Approximate educational yield is commonly stated as 36–38 ATP per glucose; actual cellular yield varies and modern accounting is often ~30–32 ATP in eukaryotes.
B **Fermentation:** anaerobic regeneration of NAD⁺. Yeast produces ethanol + CO₂; muscles under oxygen limitation produce lactate.
CORR Source equation clarified|The lecture equation reverses products in places. Aerobic respiration **consumes O₂ and releases CO₂ + H₂O while conserving energy in ATP**.

H2 Water relations and movement
ADDED Added-scope marker|The cohesion–tension, water-potential and stomatal-control synthesis below is assistant-added to connect the source xylem material with supplied PYQs.
B Water potential is greatest in pure water; solutes lower it. Water enters root hairs osmotically, crosses cortex via apoplast and symplast, is forced through endodermal membranes by the Casparian strip, and enters xylem.
B **Transpiration** is water-vapour loss, mainly through stomata. It cools leaves, helps mineral ascent and drives cohesion–tension flow, but excessive loss causes wilting.
B **Cohesion** holds water molecules together; **adhesion** attracts them to xylem walls. Together with negative pressure from evaporation, they maintain the water column.
B **Root pressure** can contribute, especially at night/small plants, and may cause guttation; it cannot by itself explain ascent in the tallest trees.
B Stomata are epidermal pores controlled by paired guard cells. High turgor generally opens the pore; turgor loss closes it.

H2 Nitrogen fixation and biofertilisers
B Atmospheric N₂ is unavailable to most plants until converted to ammonia/nitrate. Biological nitrogen fixation uses the oxygen-sensitive enzyme **nitrogenase**.
B **Rhizobium** forms nodules on legumes such as pea, gram and groundnut; **Frankia** associates with selected non-legumes.
B Free-living/asymbiotic fixers include Azotobacter (aerobic), Clostridium (anaerobic) and Azospirillum (associative, especially grasses/cereals).
B Cyanobacteria Anabaena and Nostoc fix nitrogen. The **Azolla–Anabaena** association is used in paddy fields.
B Mycorrhiza is a fungus–root mutualism: fungi improve mineral/water absorption, notably phosphorus, while plants supply carbohydrate; it can protect against root-borne pathogens and stress.

H2 Circulatory designs and heart wall
B William Harvey established the circulation of blood and is associated in the source with angiology. **Open circulation** releases haemolymph into sinuses, typical of arthropods and most molluscs; **closed circulation** keeps blood in vessels, typical of annelids and chordates.

H2 Heart and double circulation
B Four chambers: right atrium and ventricle carry deoxygenated blood; left atrium and ventricle carry oxygenated blood. Septa limit mixing.
B Valves ensure one-way flow: tricuspid (right AV), mitral/bicuspid (left AV), pulmonary semilunar and aortic semilunar.
B Flow: body → venae cavae → right atrium → tricuspid → right ventricle → pulmonary valve/artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral → left ventricle → aortic valve/aorta → body.
B **Pulmonary artery** carries deoxygenated blood and **pulmonary veins** oxygenated blood—the key vessel-name exceptions. Vessel names are based on direction relative to heart, not oxygen content.
H3 Conducting system and cycle
B Sinoatrial (SA) node initiates the heartbeat and is the normal pacemaker; atrioventricular (AV) node delays conduction; bundle of His and Purkinje fibres distribute it through ventricles.

B “Lub” is mainly closure of AV valves; “dub” closure of semilunar valves.
B Blood pressure is written systolic/diastolic; a conventional textbook reference is ~120/80 mm Hg, not a universal personal target. A **sphygmomanometer** measures it. Persistent elevation is hypertension; tachycardia is increased heart rate and bradycardia decreased rate.
B **Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume**; textbook resting values are about 72/min × 70 mL ≈ 5 L/min. A cardiac cycle at 72/min lasts about 0.8 s.
B ECG: P wave—atrial depolarisation; QRS—ventricular depolarisation; T—ventricular repolarisation.

H2 Mechanics of breathing
B Inspiration at rest: diaphragm contracts/flattens and external intercostals lift ribs → thoracic volume rises → intrapulmonary pressure falls → air enters.
B Expiration at rest is mostly passive: muscles relax and elastic recoil reduces thoracic volume, raising pressure. Forced expiration uses internal intercostals and abdominal muscles.
B **Surfactant** from type-II alveolar cells lowers surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse.
B Intrapleural pressure remains negative relative to atmospheric pressure in normal breathing, helping keep lungs expanded.
IMG breathing_mechanics:Pressure and volume changes during inhalation and exhalation|60

H2 Gas exchange and transport
B Alveoli provide enormous area, thin diffusion barrier, moist surface and rich capillary network. O₂ diffuses down partial-pressure gradient into blood; CO₂ diffuses oppositely.
B Most O₂ travels reversibly bound to haemoglobin as oxyhaemoglobin; a small fraction dissolves in plasma.
B CO₂ travels chiefly as bicarbonate, with smaller fractions as carbaminohaemoglobin and dissolved gas. Carbonic anhydrase in RBCs accelerates CO₂ + H₂O ↔ H₂CO₃ ↔ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.
B Tissue conditions with higher CO₂/H⁺/temperature promote oxygen unloading (Bohr effect). Oxygenation of haemoglobin in lungs facilitates CO₂ release (Haldane effect).

H2 Bacteria, viruses, viroids and prions
B Antonie van Leeuwenhoek observed bacteria/“animalcules” in the 17th century. **Bergey's Manual of Determinative Bacteriology** is a classic identification/classification reference.
B Bacterial nutrition is metabolically diverse: photoautotrophs use light (e.g., purple/green sulphur bacteria), chemoautotrophs oxidise inorganic compounds, saprotrophs decompose dead matter, and parasites/pathogens feed in living hosts.
B Useful activities retained from the source: ammonification; nitrification by Nitrosomonas/Nitrobacter; nitrogen fixation; curd/fermentation and vinegar by Acetobacter; fibre retting; vitamin/antibiotic production; E. coli as an indicator of faecal water contamination; E. coli and Agrobacterium as genetic-engineering workhorses.
B The term **antibiotic** is associated with S. A. Waksman; streptomycin from *Streptomyces griseus* was the first antibiotic discovered from a bacterium/actinomycete in the source framing.
B A virus is acellular genetic material (DNA **or** RNA) inside a protein capsid, sometimes with an envelope. It is inert/crystallisable outside cells and an obligate intracellular parasite inside. Plant viruses are often RNA viruses; bacteriophages infect bacteria.
B Discovery milestones: Ivanowsky linked a filterable agent to tobacco mosaic (1892); Beijerinck called it *contagium vivum fluidum* (1898); W. M. Stanley crystallised TMV (1935).
B **Viroid:** small naked infectious RNA, discovered by T. O. Diener. **Prion:** infectious misfolded protein causing transmissible neurodegeneration.
CORR Source terminology|The source prints “prisons”; the intended word is **prions**. A virus is not a nucleoprotein in every simplistic structural sense—enveloped viruses also contain lipid membrane and accessory proteins.

H2 Antibiotics and antimicrobial resistance
B Antibiotics target bacterial structures/processes such as cell-wall synthesis, ribosomes, DNA metabolism or folate pathways. They do not treat ordinary viral infections.
B Penicillin was observed by Alexander Fleming (1928); Florey and Chain helped develop it therapeutically. Penicillins inhibit bacterial cell-wall synthesis.
B Chloramphenicol inhibits the bacterial 50S ribosomal subunit. Quinine is an antimalarial drug, not an antibiotic. Sulfonamides are synthetic antibacterial agents and are often distinguished from classic microbe-derived antibiotics.
B Resistance arises through mutation or acquisition of resistance genes and is selected by misuse/overuse. Complete prescribed courses as clinically directed; avoid self-medication and use diagnostics/stewardship.

H2 Ecosystem components and energy
B Abiotic: light, temperature, water, air, soil and mineral nutrients. Biotic: **producers, consumers and decomposers**.

H2 Adaptations and behavioural ecology
ADDED Added-scope marker|This adaptation summary is assistant-added to connect ecology/evolution PYQs with common organismal examples.

H2 How to attack biology MCQs
B **Define first.** Is the stem asking discovery of substance, structure, function, site, vector, causal agent, deficiency or exception?
B **Mark universals.** Words such as always, only, all and never often signal an overstatement; seek known exceptions.
B **Separate process levels.** Breathing ≠ cellular respiration; excretion ≠ egestion; pollination ≠ fertilisation; immunity ≠ antibiotic action.
B **Trace a pathway.** For heart, nephron, digestive tract, sperm, nerve and xylem, write the ordered route mentally.
B **Match agent to disease.** Vector transmits; pathogen causes; reservoir maintains; vaccine prevents; antibiotic targets bacteria.
B **Use option granularity.** Prefer the more specific answer if it does not contradict a broader one—unless both appear as separate overlapping choices, when the item may be defective.
B **Respect official keys but learn corrections.** The PYQ appendix preserves the supplied answer and a study answer/status so you can distinguish exam history from science.
